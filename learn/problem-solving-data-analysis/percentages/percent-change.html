Percent Change: Divide by the Original

Watch a 40 → 50 price jump become +25%, dodge the divide-by-the-new-value trap, and see why +10% then −10% never cancels.

Two bars, one rule: divide the change by the ORIGINAL. Drag the new bar yourself, hit percent targets, and walk away with the multiplier shortcut the SAT rewards.

Percent change measures a jump relative to where it started: subtract the old value from the new one, then divide by the ORIGINAL and multiply by 100. A price going from 40 to 50 is a +25% increase (10/40 = 0.25), which is the same as multiplying by 1.25. The most common SAT mistake is dividing by the new value instead of the original.

Key takeaways

  • The change is new − old: The raw change is new − old: 50 − 40 = 10. The sign carries direction — positive is an increase, negative is a decrease.
  • Divide by the ORIGINAL: Percent change = (new − old) / old × 100%. The denominator is always the old value — the base: 10/40 = +25%. Exam tip: SAT wording: 'increased by p%' means multiply by (1 + p/100); 'decreased by p%' means ×(1 − p/100).
  • The wrong-base trap: Dividing by the NEW value gives 10/50 = 20% — plausible and wrong. The base is where you STARTED, and on multiple-choice questions the SAT plants this exact wrong answer in the choices.
  • Direction and chains: 40 → 50 is +25% but 50 → 40 is only −20% — the base changed. And chains multiply: +10% then −10% = ×1.1 × 0.9 = ×0.99, never a wash. Exam tip: To undo a +p% change, divide by (1 + p/100) — do not subtract p% of the new total.

Lesson transcript

  1. A price goes from 40 to 50. Everyone can see it went up by 10 — but how big is that jump, really? Is it a big deal or barely noticeable? The raw number 10 cannot tell you on its own. Percent change is the tool that answers this, and the SAT tests it constantly. Watch the two bars.
  2. First, the raw change: new minus old. 50 minus 40 is 10 — that is this green segment sticking out past the old bar. The sign carries direction: positive means an increase, negative means a decrease. But 10 by itself is not the answer. We need to compare it to something.
  3. Here is the whole rule. To turn that 10 into a percent, divide it by the ORIGINAL value — the old bar, the base. 10 divided by 40 is 0.25, so the price rose 25 percent. Percent change is always change over original, times 100. The denominator is where you STARTED, every single time.
  4. There is a second way to say the same thing: a plus 25 percent change means the new value is 1.25 times the old one. Check it — 40 times 1.25 is exactly 50. That multiplier, 1 plus p over 100, is the fastest tool on the SAT: one multiplication instead of compute, then add.
  5. Now the classic trap. Divide by the NEW number instead, and you get 10 over 50 — 20 percent. It looks plausible, and it is wrong: 50 was never the starting point. On multiple-choice percent questions, the SAT writes a choice for exactly this mistake. The base is always the original — watch the highlight snap back where it belongs.
  6. Your turn. Both bars start at 60 — old and new are equal, so the change is zero. Grab the right edge of the new bar, or use the slider, and stretch it until the readout shows exactly plus 25 percent. Think first: 25 percent of 60 is how much?
  7. Now run it backwards: a price goes from 50 down to 40. The change is minus 10 — but the base is now 50, because 50 is where THIS move started. Minus 10 over 50 is minus 20 percent. Not minus 25. Going up from 40 to 50 was plus 25 percent; coming back down is only minus 20 — because the base changed.
  8. Last trap: chains. Start at 100, go up 10 percent — times 1.1 — and you land at 110. Now drop 10 percent. But 10 percent of 110 is 11, not 10, so you fall to 99. Plus 10 then minus 10 is times 1.1 times 0.9 — times 0.99 overall, a net 1 percent loss. Successive changes multiply; they never just cancel.
  9. Time to lock it in. The old bar is fixed at 40. I will call out a percent change — you drag the new bar until the readout matches. Three rounds: up 50 percent, down 25 percent, then double it. Use the rule: take that percent of 40, then add or subtract.
  10. You now own the one formula this whole skill runs on: percent change equals new minus old, over the ORIGINAL, times 100. 40 to 50 is plus 25 percent — times 1.25 — and you never divide by the new number. I have saved this picture, turned it into 6 flashcards, and lined up what comes next.

Frequently asked questions

Why divide by the original and not the new value?

Percent change measures the jump relative to where it STARTED. From 40 to 50 the jump is 10, and 10/40 = 25%. Dividing by the new value (10/50 = 20%) answers a different question — and on multiple-choice questions it is the most common wrong answer the SAT plants in the choices.

What is the percent change formula?

Percent change = (new − old) / old × 100%. Keep the sign: a positive result is an increase, a negative result is a decrease. The denominator is always the original value.

What does a multiplier like ×1.25 mean?

A +25% change makes the new value 1.25 times the old one: new = old × (1 + 25/100). It is the same fact as the formula, compressed into one multiplication — 40 × 1.25 = 50.

Is +10% then −10% back to the start?

No. The factors multiply: ×1.1 then ×0.9 is ×0.99 overall — a net 1% loss. The −10% acts on the larger base of 110, so it removes 11, not 10. Successive percent changes never just cancel.

Why is 40 → 50 a +25% increase but 50 → 40 only a −20% decrease?

Because the base changed. Going up, the change of 10 is measured against 40: +25%. Coming down, the same 10 is measured against 50: −20%. Up and down by the same amount are NOT the same percent.

How are percent change answers entered on the SAT?

This skill appears as SPR (grid-in) questions: for a 'what percent' answer, enter 25, not 0.25 and not 25%. The built-in Desmos calculator can do the arithmetic, but choosing the right base is on you.
